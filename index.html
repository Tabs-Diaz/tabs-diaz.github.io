Why do the lights keep going out in Puerto Rico?

If you hear about outages through family calls, group chats, and social media, this is the story behind them: what happened, what the numbers can and can't tell us, and who is building something better.

This project started with my sister. I grew up in New York City, and in all my years there I lived through only two major blackouts: the Northeast blackout in 2003 and Hurricane Sandy in 2012. Two. Each one felt like a once-in-a-generation event. Then, in 2020, my sister moved to Puerto Rico, and I saw a very different reality. Blackouts and brownouts weren’t events there. They were routine. And alongside them came some of the highest electricity bills I had ever seen. Imagine paying that much for power that might not even stay on. That’s the tension. Hurricane Maria in 2017 exposed just how vulnerable the grid was. In 2021, LUMA Energy took over operation of the transmission and distribution system, and you’d think that would make things clearer. But from the outside, it’s still hard to tell how the system is actually performing, because public outage data is limited and inconsistent. So where does the story go? I’m not here to claim one magic fix. I’m asking a bigger, more hopeful question: what could a resilient, locally driven energy future look like, and what can the diaspora do to help build it?

Start with the history

A grid under strain long before Maria

For decades, one government-owned utility, the Puerto Rico Electric Power Authority (), ran the whole system. It carried heavy debt and aging equipment, which made maintenance hard to sustain.

  1. 2017

    PREPA enters restructuring under . In September, Hurricane Maria causes one of the longest large-scale outages in modern U.S. history. A study in the New England Journal of Medicine estimated deaths after the storm (Kishore et al., 2018).

  2. 2019

    Act 17 sets a legal path to much more renewable energy and an end to coal.

  3. 2021

    LUMA Energy takes over under a 15-year contract. Generation stays under separate arrangements, so responsibility for your relative's electricity is split across several entities.

  4. 2022

    Hurricane Fiona causes widespread outages, while localized solar microgrids (such as in Adjuntas) demonstrate resilient backup power.

What the numbers show, and what they don't

Reliability data does exist, but it comes in different measures, years, and definitions. Here is what we can say, and where the picture gets blurry.

In 2025, the average customer in Puerto Rico went without power for about 59 hours. The average U.S. customer: about 10.

Over time, the story is bumpier than "better" or "worse." Outages peaked in 2022, then fell, but they haven't dropped steadily since. The 2020 value of zero may reflect missing data rather than a year without outages, which is exactly why we don't draw a before-and-after conclusion from this one chart.

About 93% of the island's generating capacity is fossil fuel, and solar is only about 167 megawatts. That is , so read it as a snapshot.

Missing or changed data isn't proof of mismanagement. It could mean different definitions, new technology, or less transparency, and we don't assume which.

What a more resilient grid could look like

No single technology fixes this. Here are the approaches being tried, and what each can realistically offer.

  • Solar

    Cuts reliance on imported fuel. Needs the grid to carry it.

  • Batteries

    Store power so critical services keep running when the lines go down.

  • Community systems

    Local, small-scale power that keeps neighborhoods lit and puts decisions closer to residents.
